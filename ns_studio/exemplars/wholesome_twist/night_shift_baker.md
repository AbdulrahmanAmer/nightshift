On the first night of December, the man in the gray coat did not come, and Dina stood at the counter staring at the empty shelf where his bag waited.

She had baked at the corner shop on Pell Street for four years, from ten at night until six in the morning, and the work suited her. The ovens hummed, the dough rose quietly under its cloths, and nobody asked her anything. The man had first appeared in March, at ten past three, in the rain. He ordered six plain rolls and paid in exact coins, and he never ate one, and he never sat down. He simply took the bag and walked out, and the bell over the door rang twice behind him.

Dina was a patient woman, but nobody can watch the same stranger for nine months without inventing theories.

Her first theory was a large family, except that he wore no ring and never mentioned home. Her second was that he sold the rolls to someone else for more money, which seemed like a lot of trouble for plain bread. Her third theory she liked least of all. Every night he checked the street before stepping outside, first left and then right, like a man with something to hide. She wondered what unusual secret requires six rolls at exactly the same hour.

She imagined a person sleeping in a cellar, and she imagined a terrible debt, and she lay awake through the afternoons sorting the possibilities, each one darker than the last.

In September she stopped wondering and followed him.

It was foolish, and she knew it. She set the loaves in the proofing racks, locked the front door, and walked half a block behind his gray coat. He turned onto Cobb Road, past the enormous concrete laundry that never closed, where steam leaked from the vents all night and the windows glowed yellow. Then he disappeared down a narrow alley that smelled of hot cotton and soap, and Dina stopped at the corner and peered around a dumpster.

At the back of the building was a loading dock with one bare bulb above it. Six people sat on crates and upturned buckets in rubber boots and damp aprons, the night crew of the laundry. They stood up together when the man arrived, the way people stand when a late bus finally comes. He gave each of them a roll without speaking, and a tall woman held hers in both hands like something that could break.

"Same as always," the woman said.

"Same as always," said the man.

Then they ate in the cold and the steam, and for ten minutes nobody on that dock was tired. Dina watched until her feet went numb, and then she hurried back to the shop and rescued her loaves just in time. Her theories had all been wrong, but she still did not understand the reason, and that bothered her more than the theories ever had.

The answer came on the last night of November. The man did not arrive at ten past three, and at dawn she found an envelope pushed under the door, with a single sheet folded very small inside it.

"To the baker," it read. "I am moving to my sister's town tomorrow, so these are my last six rolls. You deserve to know why. I worked nights at that laundry for thirty years, the pay was thin, and nobody remembered us. Then a baker named Hal, who owned this shop before you, began leaving a bag at our dock every night. There was no note and no reason. He did it until the day he retired, and when he stopped, I decided somebody ought to carry it on. That was March. It has been the best money I ever spent. Your bread is better than his, but please don't tell him. Same as always, Gray Coat."

Dina read it twice. Then she folded it along its creases and tucked it into her apron pocket, beside the flour scoop.

On the first night of December, the shelf at the back of the shop was empty at ten o'clock. By three it held a paper bag, and in the bag were six plain rolls, still warm.

She locked the door and checked the street, first left and then right. Then she walked to the laundry to find out whether anyone still came for them, and the tall woman looked up from her crate and smiled.

"Same as always," Dina said.

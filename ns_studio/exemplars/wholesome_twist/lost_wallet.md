I took a hundred and ninety dollars from a stranger's wallet once, and I was sorry about it for eleven years.

I was twenty years old, cleaning offices through the night and sleeping through the afternoons, and my rent was due on a Friday. On Thursday morning I found a brown leather wallet on the back seat of the number 14 bus, as soft as old bread. The left fold held two hundred and twelve dollars in loose bills, and the right fold held a plain white envelope and a library card with an address.

I rode to the end of the line with that wallet in my coat pocket, telling myself all the usual comfortable things. Somebody lost it, somebody will cancel the cards, and somebody will certainly be fine. Then I pictured my landlord standing at my apartment door with his arms crossed, and I counted out a hundred and ninety dollars and slid it into my sock.

I slept terribly, and by evening the money felt warm against my ankle, as if it had a pulse of its own. So I tore a page from my work schedule and wrote on the back of it, "I took 190 dollars. I am sorry, and I will pay it back. I clean at Harlow Tower, nights. My name is Ines Calder." I tucked the note into the wallet and walked across the city to the address on the library card.

It was a narrow house with a blue gate. An old woman opened the door in a gray cardigan, small and remarkably straight, and I held out the wallet and waited for the shouting. She looked at me for what felt like a full minute, and eventually she said, "Thank you," and closed the door. I stood on her step feeling like a person who had been weighed and found light.

I paid my rent. On the first of every month after that, I mailed twenty dollars to the house with the blue gate, along with a card that said, "Still working on it." Every envelope came back unopened, and on each one she had written a single word in pencil, which was No. I took it for anger. After nine months I stopped trying, but I kept the envelopes in a shoebox, because throwing them away felt like another kind of lie.

Years passed, and I saved enough to open a bicycle repair shop on Fenn Street, trying to be a person who gives back more than she takes. Last March, a woman in a green coat wheeled a bicycle with a flat tire through my door, studying my face as if comparing it to a photograph. "Are you Ines Calder?" she asked, and my stomach dropped all the way down to my sock.

"My mother was Wilhelmina Voss, from the blue gate," she said. "She passed away in January, and she kept your note in this wallet for eleven years." She set it on my counter, as soft as old bread.

I couldn't speak, so she kept going. Her mother had left that wallet on a bus seat every Friday for six years, with about two hundred dollars in the left fold and an envelope in the right. Inside the envelope was a card that I had never opened. It said, "If you need some of this, take what you need. Then bring the wallet back and tell me the truth about it, so I know that somebody did."

"Most people kept everything and said nothing," the woman told me. "You were the only one who walked up to her door and told her exactly what you took."

"I never even read the card," I said.

"That's what she liked best. You didn't need it." She almost smiled. "She refused your envelopes because it was never a loan, and she said you paid the whole debt the moment you knocked."

I opened the wallet with hands that did not feel like mine. The left fold held one hundred and ninety dollars in crisp bills, and beside it was a pencil note in her small, upright writing: "For the next one."

I repaired her tire without charging anything, and she graciously allowed it. On Friday I rode the 14 bus to the end of the line and set the wallet on the back seat with the envelope in the right fold. Then I got off one stop early, so I wouldn't have to watch.

I took a hundred and ninety dollars from a stranger once. It turns out she left it there for me, and now I finally know how to pay it back.

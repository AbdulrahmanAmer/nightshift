The radio starts at nine, and you have hated it since the very first night.

You moved into your father's house in October, with eleven boxes and an unreliable back, and the house was unexpectedly quiet. Next door lives a man named Teodor, thin and slightly stooped, with a gray mustache. He has the shuffle of a person who has somewhere to be and nobody to go with. You have decided that he is trouble, because you are good at reading people, and you always have been.

Here is what you have noticed, and it is perfectly simple: every night at nine he carries an enormous wooden radio onto his porch. It is the size of a bread box, with a cracked dial and a cloth speaker the color of tea. He sets it on the rail and turns it toward your kitchen window. Out comes an electric crackle, then a murmur of music, then a man's voice describing the temperature in places you have never heard of. Teodor sits down in the darkness with his arms folded and stares steadily at your house.

You wrote the times in your notebook: nine on Monday, nine on Tuesday, and nine on Wednesday, even in the rain.

It does not take a detective to understand this. A man who aims a speaker at a window wants to know when a person is home or asleep, and he wants something, because men like that always do. Perhaps it is the house, or perhaps he hopes you will become discouraged and leave. In the first week he knocked, and you did not answer, because you saw him through the curtain holding a covered casserole and you knew exactly what that meant. People bring you food so that you will owe them.

So you wrote a note that said, "Please stop pointing your radio at my house." You pressed so hard that the pen went through the paper, and you slid it under his door on Friday afternoon and felt a small, private satisfaction.

At nine that night the radio did not play. You stood listening to nothing, and the nothing was enormous, because the refrigerator hummed and a branch scratched the glass and you waited, though you would never admit it. You did not fall asleep until three.

On Saturday you looked through the curtain, and Teodor was on his porch at nine with no radio at all, sitting very still with his hands on his knees. Now you notice something you somehow missed in a month of careful observation. There are two chairs on his porch, and one of them is empty, and on its arm hangs a flat tweed cap that you would recognize anywhere.

Your legs carry you out the door and across the wet grass before your mind has voted. Teodor stands when he sees you coming, looking as if he might apologize for being alive.

"I got your note, and I am sorry, and I will keep it off," he says.

"Why is that cap on your porch?" you ask.

He looks at it and then at you, and his mustache trembles. "Your father's. He sat in that chair for thirty-one years, and every night at nine we listened to the evening broadcast. On Fridays we played chess, and he always cheated shamelessly." He swallows. "The radio was his, you understand. When his hands became too unsteady to tune it, he carried it over here and said, Teodor, you keep it going, so I keep it going."

You cannot find a single word.

"I pointed it at your window because the signal is better that way," he says. "And I suppose because that kitchen light was his, and I thought if the music drifted over, the house would not feel so empty. I should have asked permission, but I knocked once, and you did not answer, and I did not know how to try again."

The dish on his table, you realize, is still there under a cloth, waiting for somebody to ask what it is.

"Plum cake," he says, following your eyes. "He liked it with salt."

You laugh, and it comes out wrong, and then it keeps coming, while Teodor stands there completely helpless, holding out a handkerchief, which you accept. Over his shoulder, just inside the door, the radio sits on a shelf, silent and ordinary, with its dial glowing the color of tea.

"Please turn it on," you say.

The radio starts at nine. Tonight you carry the second chair out yourself.

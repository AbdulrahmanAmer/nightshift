The book came through the return slot at eight in the morning, carrying a card that was older than the slot itself. I was the only person in the building, so I knelt on the carpet and examined it carefully. It was a field guide to coastal birds, with a cloth cover worn as soft as an old glove.

The card in the back pocket was the original kind, with a column for names and a column for dates. Only one line had ever been completed, and the due date was stamped in purple ink, the fourteenth of March, 1978. The four in that date had a small white nick in it, like a chipped tooth. I recognized the nick immediately, because I had made it myself.

In the spring of 1978 I was nineteen years old and shelving books for a little pocket money. Miss Pruitt, the head librarian, allowed me to use the date stamp on quiet afternoons, and one day I dropped it on the tile floor. The four chipped, and she promised to order a replacement, but she never did, and for years afterward every date I printed carried that little scar. So the book was mine, in a certain sense, because I had been the one who checked it out.

Here is everything I remember about that particular afternoon. A tall, solemn man in a work jacket came in around closing time, with machine oil under his fingernails. He stood at the desk for a long time before he asked, very quietly, for a book about birds, the way people ask for something they are embarrassed to want. I found this one for him, and when I slid the card across to be signed, his hand was trembling noticeably.

The name on the card is not in cursive. Every letter stands alone, in deliberate capitals with space around each one, like a child's first attempt at writing, and they spelled DANIEL HOLT. I noticed it that evening and understood the whole situation without being told. I said nothing, stamped the date, and handed him the book, and he promised to bring it back, and I told him to take his time.

That is the entire memory, and I had not considered it in several decades until this morning.

I read the card again, slowly, and saw what I had missed on the first reading. Underneath the old line was a new one, written in blue ballpoint, in smooth joined letters. Daniel Holt, it said, and the date beside it was last Tuesday. It was the same name in a completely different hand, because the hand had finally learned how to move.

I sat back on my heels and realized that the man had signed twice, forty-eight years apart, and the second signature was simply the first one, grown up.

At ten o'clock the door opened, and a woman in her fifties came in holding a folded paper. She had a long jaw and exhausted eyes, and she stood at the desk exactly the way he had stood, as though the room might push back.

"I left a book in your slot," she said. "I was not certain anybody would notice."

"I noticed," I told her. "Is your father well?"

She shook her head gently, and said, "He passed away on Thursday. He kept that book beside his bed for most of my life. He said a girl at the library once taught him to read from it, but he never explained how, and I assumed it was only a story."

"It was a very short lesson," I said. "Mainly he taught himself. I only wrote the date on a card."

"He practiced his name on the back of every envelope in the house," she said. "Years and years of those enormous deliberate letters. But last week he asked me for a pen, and he wrote it one more time, like this." She unfolded the paper, and there was the same smooth line of ink. "He told me, now it can go back."

I took the book from her, and my eyes stung, and I let them.

The date stamp was in my drawer, the same stamp with the same chipped four, because nobody after Miss Pruitt had ever ordered a new one either. I inked it carefully and set it above the old purple line.

The book had come through the slot, and now it was home. I pressed down, and the little nick printed as clean as it ever had.

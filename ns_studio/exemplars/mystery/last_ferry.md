The ferry leaves at six, and it will not wait for the truth. Constable Tamsin Reeve understood that the moment the call came, at ten minutes to five. Somebody had deliberately stolen Old Ansel's fiddle from the Harbor Hall, and the thief had only one way off the island.

She had seventy minutes. The fiddle was a hundred and ten years old, the pride of the island, and tonight was the closing of the autumn fair. Ansel had played it at four, then set it in its case on the stage and gone off to shake hands. When he returned at a quarter to five, the case was latched and mysteriously light. The velvet inside was empty, and nobody had noticed anything, although two hundred people had been in the hall.

With forty-five minutes left, Tamsin stood at the gangway beside the deckhand, Corin, holding a clipboard. The remaining passengers came down the pier in ones and twos, carrying pie tins and folding chairs. She examined every bag and every coat, and she made a tall man open his accordion case, which he did not appreciate at all. Corin counted the passengers carefully, twenty-two, then twenty-three, and still there was no fiddle.

At thirty minutes she walked back to the hall to think. It was deserted now, and it smelled of wet wool and cider. The stage was bare except for a piano and a mop bucket. She sat down on the piano bench and pressed a single key, hoping to hear something besides her own pulse. The note gave a flat, unpleasant buzz, like a bee trapped in a jar.

She frowned, because the piano had sounded absolutely perfect at four. She remembered the opening waltz, clear and bright as a bell. Then she remembered the piano tuner, Gus Farrow, who had worked on it all week and had announced at the gangway that he was staying the night. He was a quiet, unremarkable man, and he had not carried a bag at all.

Twenty minutes remained when she checked her list again. Everyone leaving had a perfectly ordinary explanation for being there, everyone except Gus. She opened the ticket book and found his name. He had bought a ticket that very morning and returned it at four, and the line was crossed out in pencil. A person who changes his mind before the fair is over has either a good reason or a plan.

With twelve minutes to spare, Tamsin ran immediately. The hall door stood ajar, though she had closed it herself. She slowed carefully at the threshold and heard a soft sound from the stage, like a wooden lid being lifted. Gus knelt at the open piano with a tuning wrench in his hand. Inside, wrapped in a gray felt cloth and lying across the strings, was a violin.

"I only moved it," he said. "I thought it would be safer."

"Safer from whom?" she asked. "You put it there at ten past four, while Ansel was shaking hands. You meant to wait until the ferry had gone, and then you would walk out at your leisure, while the entire island searched the pier."

Gus looked down at the strings. The buzz had given him away, because a violin laid across them made every note above middle C rattle, and he must have known it from the very first chord.

Four minutes before six, she lifted the precious instrument in both arms and said nothing more. She escorted him down the lane, past the lamps coming on one by one. At the end of the pier, Ansel stood with his hat in his hands, and she gave the fiddle back to him. The old man did not speak either. He only pressed his cheek against the wood, the way a person greets an old friend.

Two minutes later, Corin released the rope and the engine coughed twice and caught. Along the rail, twenty-three passengers leaned out to wave goodbye, still holding their pie tins and their chairs, and not one of them knew how close it had been.

At six o'clock the ferry left, and the horn sounded over the water, and nothing aboard was stolen.

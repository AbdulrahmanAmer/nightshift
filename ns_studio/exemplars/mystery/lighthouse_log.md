The page was gone, and the cut was so clean that Inspector Pell almost admired it. He discovered the gap at nine in the morning, on the first day of his yearly visit to Gull Point light. The logbook held three hundred and sixty-four nights in the same careful handwriting, with every seven crossed in the old-fashioned way. Only the night of the fourteenth of October had been sliced out close to the spine.

Edda Marsh, the keeper, stood beside the kettle and offered no explanation. She was sixty-eight years old and had tended this light for thirty-one years, and the book claimed the lamp had never once gone dark. Pell did not believe a lamp could look after itself. A missing night usually meant an absent keeper, and an absent keeper meant an extremely complicated report.

He turned back one page, looking for anything unusual or unexplained. The entry for the thirteenth ended with a complaint about her knee, worse again, and a note that one hundred and twelve steps was a long way to climb. Then he noticed the drying rack beside the sink, where two cups stood upside down, although Edda lived entirely alone.

That is how the morning ended, and to understand how it began, we must go back a few hours.

At dawn Edda sat at the kitchen table with the missing page spread out in front of her. She had cut it out herself with her late husband's razor, because the handwriting on it was not hers. It was large and rough and written in a hurry, and none of the sevens had crosses. Lamp lit at twenty to seven, it said. Fog thick by nine. Horn heard to the south at eleven. Underneath was a signature, Wilfred Oates.

Edda knew the regulations by heart. Only a certified keeper may sign the log of a working light, and a neighbor's name in that book would cost her the position, her only home. She held the page above the stove for a long minute. Then she folded it small and pushed it into her coat pocket, because burning it felt too much like burning him.

Go back further, to midnight, when the fog had settled over the point like a heavy lid.

Wilf Oates, who had once fished these waters and now only sold coal, stood in the lamp room with a cleaning rag in his hand. Far out on the water, a boat's horn sounded repeatedly. It was the Kittiwake, with three men aboard, feeling its way toward rocks they could not see. Wilf wound the clockwork and polished the great lens exactly as Edda had shown him years ago, over tea, just in case. Gradually the horn drew closer, turned away, and finally fell silent in the safe water behind the point.

Go back once more, to dusk, when Edda was halfway up the stairs with the matches.

Her knee gave out without any warning. She sat down hard on the sixtieth step and could not rise again. Wilf, who had come that afternoon to deliver coal, heard her call and found her there. She told him to fetch the doctor, but he answered that the doctor lived thirty miles away and the sun was nearly gone. She looked at the window above them, already turning gray, and then she looked at him. Neither of them mentioned the regulations, because neither of them needed to.

"Give me the matches," he said.

He climbed the last fifty-two steps alone, and at twenty to seven the great light swung out across the water, precisely on schedule.

Now come forward again, to the morning and the Inspector at the kitchen table.

Pell closed the book and held it toward the window, where the low sun slid across the paper. On the page after the missing one, faint impressions showed where a heavy pen had pressed through. He could just make out a number seven with no cross on it.

He looked at the two cups, and then he looked at Edda, whose hand rested over her coat pocket. He thought about the Kittiwake and its three men, comfortable in their beds, who would never know which hand had kept them from the rocks. Pell took out his pen and wrote his own entry. Log inspected, light in good order, no faults found. Then he handed the book back to her. The page was gone, and the cut was clean, and the lamp had not missed a single night.

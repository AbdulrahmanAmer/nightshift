The dryer at the end of the row ran by itself every night at two.

Ruth had worked the overnight shift at the Calder Street laundromat for six years, and she knew every sound the place made. She knew the shudder of the big washers in their spin and the tick of the fluorescent tubes as they warmed. She knew the sigh of the front door when a customer came in out of the rain, but dryer nine made none of those ordinary sounds. It made only a soft, slow turning, like someone rolling over in bed.

The first time, she assumed that somebody had left a coin in the slot. She walked down the row with her keys in her hand and looked through the round glass door. Inside, a single shirt tumbled over and over. It was a blue work shirt with a pocket, and it was remarkably warm when she pulled it out, although nobody had put it there.

She folded it and left it on the counter, and nobody ever claimed it. That was fine. Lost things were part of the job. The shelf behind the register already held a hundred unmatched socks, a pair of reading glasses, and a wedding veil that nobody had returned for.

The second night, dryer nine ran at two again. This time it held a gray cardigan with one button missing, and Ruth hung it on the shelf with the other strays. She did not think much of it, because machines misbehaved and the landlord had not repaired the electrical wiring since the spring.

On Thursday an electrician named Dale came in for his weekly wash, wearing a blue work shirt with a pocket. It was not the same shirt, since hers was still folded on the counter, but it was the same kind. Ruth told herself that blue shirts were common. Dale did not come in the next Thursday.

She noticed, and then she made herself stop noticing, because people moved away, and people changed laundromats for no reasonable reason at all.

That Saturday, dryer nine gave her a pale yellow plastic raincoat. The week after, it gave her a tan scarf. Ruth remembered, with a small cold feeling in her wrists, that a retired teacher named Hollis had worn exactly that scarf on her final visit. Hollis had not been seen since.

So Ruth started a notebook, because she was a careful woman, and careful women write things down. In one column she put the date and the item, and in the other she put the name of the regular who had last been seen wearing it. By the end of the month she had nine entries, and not one of those nine people had come back.

She did not telephone anybody, because she could not imagine the words. A dryer was folding the clothes of strangers. She would sound exhausted, and the conversation would end with someone kindly suggesting a vacation.

Instead she watched the entrance. Some nights she wanted to grab a customer by the sleeve and recommend that they wash their clothes at home in the sink. She never did, because she knew nobody would listen.

Tonight the rain was unusually heavy and the place was empty. At a quarter to two she made her tea, carried it to the uncomfortable plastic chair, and sat facing the row. The tubes ticked overhead, and a washer finished its cycle and went quiet.

At two o'clock, dryer nine began to turn.

Ruth waited patiently. She counted to three hundred, because careful women count. Then she walked down the row with her hands loose at her sides and looked through the round glass.

It was a green apron, folded in a perfectly neat square, with a name stitched on the chest in white thread. The owner had stitched it herself six years ago, on her very first night, at the kitchen table.

Ruth opened the door, and the apron was warm. She held it against her chest the way you would hold a cat. Then she looked down at the apron she was already wearing. The small brown stain on its pocket matched the stain on the one in her hands.

She went back to the chair and did not lock the front door. She arranged the folded apron across her knees and sat very still, listening to the machinery behind her, as if it still had more to give.

At two the next night, the dryer at the end of the row began to turn, and for the first time Ruth was not the one who went to look.

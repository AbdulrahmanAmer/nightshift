The doorbell rang at ten past three, and nobody was there.

That was how it started, on my first night in the new house. I came down the stairs in my socks with one hand on the wall, and I looked through the little glass eye in the front door. The porch light showed me a welcome mat, a dead geranium in a pot, and the empty street beyond. I opened the door anyway, and somewhere in the distance a dog barked twice and stopped.

I told myself the wiring was old, because the house had been cheap for a reason, and I had decided that the reason was dampness.

The second night it rang at ten past three again. I stood behind the door and waited, counting to a hundred, but I never heard footsteps on the boards or saw a shape against the curtain. Afterward I lay awake until the window turned gray.

On the third night I bought a camera, a small white one with a lens like a pearl. I taped it above the door and pointed it down the garden path. My brother laughed when I told him, because he knew nobody else who would pay forty dollars to watch a gate. He stopped laughing when I explained that the doorbell had no electrical wires.

I had discovered that on the fourth day, standing on a ladder with a screwdriver. Behind the brass button was a hole in the wall packed with ancient plaster, and there was no cable and no chime box anywhere in the hallway. Whatever rang that bell, nothing inside the house was helping it.

Each morning I sat at the kitchen table with a mug of tea and scrolled through the recording from the night. Path, path, path, a moth, a cat crossing at one o'clock. Then ten past three, and the bell sounding with no finger near it, and the gate standing still and innocent in the gray light.

On the fifth morning I remembered the second camera. It had come in the box as an extra for indoors, and I had connected it at the foot of the stairs without thinking about it. I had never examined its recordings, so I opened them now.

The hallway looked dim and silver on the screen. At 3:09 the top stair creaked, and a man came down in bare feet, wearing my gray shirt. He walked past the lens with his eyes half closed and his mouth a little open, the way a person walks in a dream. He unlocked the front door, stepped outside, and pulled it shut behind him.

I stopped breathing, because I recognized that walk and I recognized that shirt.

The porch camera had missed everything, since the step beneath it was a blind spot as wide as a man. The hall camera kept running, though. For forty seconds there was only the closed door and the ticking of the clock. Then the bell rang, and the door swung inward, and there I stood with my thumb still raised.

I was nodding slowly, as if somebody had said something kind. My lips moved, and although the recording had no sound, I could read them easily. Come in, I said, three times, softly and politely.

Then I stepped aside, the way you step aside for a guest with heavy luggage, and I held the door for nothing at all.

I locked up and climbed the stairs with my eyes half closed. On the video, each stair creaked a half second after my foot had left it, as if something were stepping exactly where I had stepped.

I scrolled back through the earlier nights. On the second night the creaking began at the bottom step, and on the third it began in the middle. Every night the sound had followed me a little higher, and last night it stopped outside my bedroom door and stayed there until morning.

I spent the day pushing the couch against the front door and positioning a chair under the handle. I told myself that nobody can invite something he cannot see. But then I thought of my own lips saying the words so gently, like a host who had been practicing for weeks.

It is nearly three now. I am sitting on the bottom stair with my hands tucked under my knees, wide awake, ordering my body to stay.

The doorbell rang at ten past three, and this time I was awake to feel my own thumb press the brass.

You take the last bus home, because the driver waves you on.

It is a quarter past midnight, and your feet ache from a double shift at the hotel. The bus smells of wet wool and overripe fruit, and there are only four other passengers. A woman with a shopping bag sits near the front, and two men in work boots sleep against the windows. A man in a long gray coat sits directly behind you, close enough that you can hear him breathing.

You choose not to turn around, and you press your forehead to the cold glass and watch the streetlights slide past, one orange smear after another.

Then the man leans forward, and his voice is dry and quiet, like paper being folded. He says that you must not get off at Harrow Street, and he says it as though the words cost him something.

Your heart gives a hard, unfamiliar knock. Harrow Street is your stop, and you have never seen this man before in your life. You keep your eyes on the window while your hand closes around the keys in your pocket. He asks you, very politely, to remain on the bus until the final destination.

You move to an aisle seat beside the sleeping men, telling yourself that he is drunk, or unwell, or the sort of unpleasant person who frightens strangers for entertainment. When you glance back, his face is so tired and patient that you look away immediately. His hands are folded in his lap, and his wedding ring is worn thin as wire from decades of washing.

The bus rumbles past the closed bakery, the dark petrol station, and the park with its black trees. Eventually you walk to the front, and you tell the driver that the man behind you is bothering you and that you need to get off at Harrow Street.

The driver glances in his mirror. He is old, with a gray mustache and a company cap pulled low, and he nods in a slow, knowing way that makes you uneasy. He says that he sees the man, and that you should sit down while he takes care of it.

You sit across from the woman with the shopping bag. She does not look at you, and her hands rest perfectly still on a bag that never rustles the way groceries should.

Harrow Street comes up on the right, with the shelter, the lamp, and the wooden bench where you wait on cold mornings. You rise to ring the bell, but the driver does not slow down, and the bus rolls past your stop at the same steady speed.

"That was my stop," you say, and the driver answers gently that he knows.

You turn around, and now all of them are looking at you. The men in work boots are awake, and the woman has lifted her head. The man in the gray coat is on his feet, swaying with the motion of the bus, holding a seat back for balance. He looks like someone who has run an enormous distance.

Then you see something through the rear window, past his shoulder.

Under the lamp at Harrow Street, somebody is standing. She wears your green hotel jacket, and she holds her keys exactly the way you hold yours. She is watching the bus go, and her face is turned toward the window, and it is your face, smiling with every tooth.

She starts to walk after you, never running, only walking, steady and unhurried, and the gap between her and the bus does not seem to grow.

"She waits at every stop," the man says quietly. "Every night, a little further along, she waits for someone to step off first, so that she can take their place at the door."

Your knees give way. The woman with the shopping bag moves over to make room, and her hand on your arm is cool and gentle. She explains that they all tried to get off once. He was the one who figured it out, and he has been riding for eleven years, trying to warn everybody else.

The man in the gray coat sits down across the aisle and studies you for a long moment. He tells you that it is not so bad, because you get used to the bus, and they take turns keeping watch.

You look at the clock above the driver's seat. It reads a quarter past midnight, and you realize that it has read that for some time.

So you settle back and fold your hands. Somewhere ahead, another stop is coming, with another lamp and another bench, each exactly like the last. The driver slows and opens the doors for no one, and in the mirror he finds your eyes.

Then he waves you on.

The spot was number twelve, and for two years it was never mine.

It sat beside the back door of our apartment building, only ten steps from my kitchen, and my lease said so in bold print. Every evening around six, a gray pickup slid into it as if it had been born there. I parked three streets away and carried my groceries home until the bags cut into my fingers.

The pickup belonged to Glenn, who lived in apartment 4B and had his own assigned spot at the far end of the lot, beside the dumpsters. He hated that spot, and I knew it because he told me so, twice, while standing in mine with his arms folded and his keys in his hand.

"It's only a parking space," he said. "Nobody owns the air."

I smiled at him politely, because I am the kind of person who smiles when I am furious. It is an embarrassing habit, and it turned out to be extremely useful.

My first plan was a polite note, which he put in the recycling bin, still folded, where I found it the next day. My second plan was an email to the property office, and they answered that they encourage neighbors to resolve things kindly. I printed that last word and stuck it on my refrigerator.

Then one Tuesday night I did something I had never done before, and I read the minutes of the residents' meeting. They hang beside the mailboxes, and nobody reads them, because they are written in the language of people who love committees. But on page three, in the middle of a paragraph about gutters, I found two sentences that made my heart speed up.

The first said the lot would receive a charging post for electric cars, installed right beside spot thirty-one. The second said the old oak above the back door would stay, despite many complaints, because the town protected it.

I did not own an electric car yet. My cousin was selling hers, though, and I had been thinking about it for months, so the idea suddenly felt very practical.

The next morning I read my lease again, slowly, the way you read a menu when you are broke. Page six said that any two residents could trade assigned spots, as long as both of them signed the same form. I printed the form, filled in my half, and knocked on Glenn's door.

"I've been thinking," I said. "You obviously love number twelve, and honestly, I'm tired of fighting about it. Let's make it official. You take twelve, I'll take thirty-one, and everybody wins."

He stared at me for a moment, and then he grinned, the grin of a man being handed something he had already taken, and signed immediately. I thanked him enthusiastically and gave him an official copy for his personal records.

That was in March. The oak grew its leaves in April, and in May it dropped tiny yellow flowers all over the lot. By June the tree was dripping sap, thick and sweet, that landed all night on the roof of a gray pickup, and then the hood, and then the windshield. I did not plant anything or climb anything or touch anything at all, because I simply was not there.

In July the recycling truck began backing up to number twelve at five every morning, beeping like a smoke alarm. The motion light above the door was so sensitive that a moth could trigger it, and Glenn's truck sat in white light all night like a prize.

Meanwhile, I bought my cousin's little blue car and plugged it in at the brand new charging station beside spot thirty-one. The town had moved the dumpsters in May, which made my end of the lot the quietest place in the building. I slept peacefully, I charged my electric car for pennies every evening, and I carried my groceries ten steps instead of three streets.

One evening in August, Glenn knocked on my door, holding a bucket and a scrub brush. He looked considerably older than he had in March.

"About that swap," he said. "Maybe we could switch back."

I glanced at my refrigerator, where the word kindly still hung. I tried to look sorry, and I think I almost managed it.

"Oh, Glenn, I would love to," I said. "But we both signed. And you were right, you know. It's only a parking space."

He went back downstairs to wash his windshield again, very carefully, for the third time that week.

Number twelve is still a lovely spot, with a lovely tree and a lovely view of the back door. It is still not mine, and for the first time in two years, I am completely fine with that.

Ten minutes before noon, the lunchroom at Halvorsen Supply smells like cumin, cinnamon, and trouble.

Dana stands by the door with a clipboard and counts the dishes. Twenty-two people have brought food, including rice, dumplings, a lemon cake, and a pot of beans that Hector has guarded since nine. Near the window, checking his watch, stands Marv.

Marv never brings anything except containers, which he keeps stacked in a canvas bag, and by one o'clock every single one is full of somebody else's dinner. Last year he walked out with two trays of lasagna and Dana's whole cheesecake.

This year Dana has an extremely careful plan with ten steps, and the countdown begins right now.

With ten minutes left, she tapes an enormous red card above the leftovers table. It is written in capital letters, and it is completely polite: all remaining food goes to the Eastside Pantry Drive. Marv glances at the card without any interest, then returns to checking his watch.

With nine minutes left, Hector sets out ten foil pans, each marked with a neat white sticker. Every sticker says the same thing, which is "Pantry donation, contact Marv." Marv does not notice, because Marv is looking at the cake.

With eight minutes left, Dana opens the sign-up sheet to the page nobody reads. Two months ago, at the planning meeting, someone asked who could stay late and meet the pantry van. Marv gave his big smile and said he was happy to help, and Dana wrote his name down in ink.

With seven minutes left, the first people drift toward the table, and Marv drifts faster. He opens his canvas bag, and Dana opens her mouth, thinks better of it, and closes it again.

With six minutes left, a line forms, and Marv slides to the front of it with a plate the size of a hubcap. Dana lets him, since everything on that plate is his to eat today. That is the generous and completely fair part of the plan.

With five minutes left, Dana's phone buzzes with a text from a cheerful pantry coordinator named Joan. The van has arrived early and is already waiting in the parking lot behind the building.

With four minutes left, Dana waves from the window. A tall volunteer in a green vest waves back, holding a clipboard and a stack of empty crates.

With three minutes left, Marv finds the dumplings. He is delighted, he is humming a cheerful song, and he has absolutely no idea.

With two minutes left, the volunteer walks through the door and stops. She scans the room, bright and friendly, and everyone goes quiet, because twenty-one people are suddenly very interested in their forks.

With one minute left, she speaks. "Hello, everybody. I'm looking for Marv, who I was told is our contact, and who has food for about sixty people."

Marv looks up slowly, with a dumpling halfway to his mouth, and the dumpling begins to tremble.

At noon exactly, he says the only thing anyone could possibly say, which is "That's me."

"Wonderful!" says the volunteer. "Thank you so much. The families we serve really count on people like you."

Every head in the room turns. Hector starts to clap, then Dana joins him, and soon the whole lunchroom is clapping, loud and warm, with nobody smiling harder than Dana.

Marv looks at the red card, then at the ten foil pans with his name on them, and last of all at the open canvas bag on the floor.

The volunteer hands him an empty crate and asks, "Where should we start?"

So Marv starts. He carries the rice, and he carries the beans, which Hector hands over like a trophy. He wraps the cake in foil, very slowly, and places it in the crate himself. The job takes forty minutes, his shirt turns dark under the arms, and two coworkers help him, because he is, after all, the contact.

At one o'clock the van pulls away, heavy and full. Marv sits alone in the empty lunchroom and finishes his cold dumplings. He does not say much, but he carries the plate to the sink and washes it himself.

Next June, ten minutes before noon, the lunchroom smells like cumin, cinnamon, and good things. Marv stands at the door with a pot of rice, which is a small pot, but it is his.

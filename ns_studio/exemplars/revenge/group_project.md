On the first night of the bookkeeping class, Priya bought a green notebook with a rubber band around it, though she could not yet say why she wanted it. She only knew that the instructor had announced a team project worth half of the final grade, and that a cold little feeling had settled in her stomach.

Her team had four people, and none of them had much free time. Priya worked the front desk at a dental office, Tomas drove a delivery van all day, and Wen cleaned offices at night and studied during her breaks. The fourth member was Rudy, a tall man in a very nice jacket, who sold insurance and mentioned it often.

The project was a complete financial plan for an imaginary bakery, with costs, prices, and a forecast for three years. Rudy took charge within five minutes. "I'm a big picture guy," he said. "I'll take the market research, and you three can handle the numbers."

Everyone agreed immediately, because the arrangement sounded perfectly reasonable and nobody wanted an uncomfortable argument in the first week.

In the second week, Rudy missed class and sent a message saying he was slammed at work. In the third week he arrived late with a coffee and talked for twenty minutes about the future of bread, but he brought no research. "Next week," he said. "It's all in my head."

Priya opened the green notebook and wrote the date, and under it the four names and what each person had promised. Next to Rudy she wrote: market research, due Thursday. That night she did one more small thing, and sent the group a short and friendly message. "Quick recap, Tomas has the cost sheet, Wen has the price list, I have the forecast, and Rudy has the market research, due Thursday," she wrote. "Tell me if I got anything wrong."

Nobody said she had, and Rudy even sent a cheerful thumbs-up to the whole group.

She did the same thing every week, and the recap was always polite, always short, and always a little boring. She never complained, and she never used an adjective. She simply wrote down who had promised what, and she saved every message in a folder.

Thursday came and went without any research, and so did the next one, and the one after that. Tomas finished the cost sheet at his kitchen table after midnight, and Wen built the price list on her phone between jobs. Priya wrote the forecast and checked it three times. They were tired, but the work was good, and they did not talk much about the empty chair.

In the eighth week, Rudy's research finally arrived. It was two thin paragraphs and a photograph of a croissant, so Tomas rewrote the whole thing in one night without a word of blame. Priya quietly wrote that down too.

On presentation night, Rudy announced that he would give the introduction and the key findings, since those were the talking parts. The team nodded politely, and Priya noticed with some surprise that her hands were perfectly steady.

Before the first team began, the instructor, a calm woman named Ruth Okafor, held up a single sheet of paper. "Every team will turn in a contribution record," she said. "It is one page that lists who did what, and you should attach anything that supports it."

Rudy leaned back in his chair. Later, Priya saw what he had written beside his name: leadership, research, presentation, and vision.

Priya said nothing at all, and she slipped the rubber band off the green notebook and handed over her folder of printed messages. There were eleven recaps, each with a date, and each with his own thumbs-up. Under market research she wrote three plain lines: the date he promised, the date it arrived, and the name of the person who rewrote it.

The instructor read the page for a long time, and she read it twice. Then she looked up. "Rudy," she said kindly, "which three bakeries did you study, and what did you learn from them?"

Rudy opened his mouth and said, "Well, the big ones, mostly."

The room was completely quiet, and nobody laughed, because nobody needed to.

The team earned an A. Rudy earned a C, and he never asked why, because he had approved every single message himself. His thumbs-up were all there in a row, with the dates.

Priya finished the course in the spring and passed her certificate exam on the first attempt. Tomas found a better job in a warehouse office, and Wen moved to the front counter of a small bakery, which seemed only right.

Priya still has the green notebook, with the rubber band around it. It has one page left, and she is saving it.
